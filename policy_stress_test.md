# Phase B — Synthetic Media Governance Policy
## Stress Test, Limitations, and Residual Risk

### Purpose

This document stress-tests the proposed **University Communications Office Synthetic Media Governance Policy**.

The objective is not to argue that the policy eliminates synthetic-media risk. Instead, it examines realistic situations in which the policy could be misunderstood, circumvented, become outdated, or fail to control what happens after synthetic media leaves the University's direct control.

The analysis focuses on the policy's actual controls: risk classification, consent, approved-tool requirements, disclosure, provenance, human review, change control, incident response, auditing, and refusal.

---

# 1. Employee Misunderstands a Consent Boundary

### Scenario

An employee needs a short recruitment video and finds a photograph of a university administrator on the University's public website. The employee assumes that because the photograph is already publicly available, it can be uploaded to an AI avatar platform.

The resulting avatar is intended to deliver a truthful recruitment message.

### Policy Control

The policy requires documented authorization before a recognizable person's likeness or voice is synthetically reproduced. It also distinguishes permission to use an existing photograph from permission to create a reusable synthetic likeness.

### Where the Policy Could Fail

The employee may understand the rule but incorrectly interpret public availability as permission for synthetic reproduction.

The employee might also obtain permission to use the photograph but fail to obtain permission to create a reusable avatar.

### Residual Risk

The policy still depends on human judgment at the point where source material is selected.

### Additional Control

Training should explicitly teach employees:

> Publicly available does not mean authorized for synthetic reproduction.

The pre-publication checklist should also require the reviewer to identify whether any recognizable person appears in the output.

---

# 2. Consent Is Valid but Too Narrowly Understood

### Scenario

A university administrator gives written permission for an AI avatar to appear in one admissions video.

Six months later, a different department wants to reuse the avatar in a donor campaign.

### Policy Control

The policy requires consent records to specify purpose, audience, distribution channels, duration, reuse, and revocation.

### Where the Policy Could Fail

Someone may treat the existence of the original consent record as permanent authorization.

The second department may not know that the first consent was limited to admissions.

### Residual Risk

Consent is not a one-time technical checkbox. It has scope, duration, and context.

### Additional Control

Tier 3 consent records should include a visible expiration date and a project identifier. Reuse should require a new approval when the purpose, audience, channel, or message materially changes.

---

# 3. Consent Is Revoked After Publication

### Scenario

A faculty member authorizes a synthetic avatar for a recruitment campaign and later withdraws permission.

Several versions of the campaign have already been published across university channels.

### Policy Control

The policy requires a revocation process and review of active campaigns.

### Where the Policy Could Fail

The University can remove content from channels it controls, but it cannot guarantee removal of:

- reposts;
- screenshots;
- screen recordings;
- derivative edits;
- copies hosted by third parties.

### Residual Risk

Withdrawal of consent can stop future authorized use, but it cannot guarantee the disappearance of every historical copy.

### Additional Control

Tier 3 projects should maintain a distribution inventory so the Communications Office can quickly identify all known channels when consent is withdrawn.

---

# 4. The Artifact Is Truthful but Misrepresented After Distribution

### Scenario

The Communications Office publishes a correctly labeled synthetic research video.

A third party extracts a short section, removes the surrounding disclosure, and posts the clip with a caption implying that the depicted person personally made the statement.

### Policy Control

The policy requires disclosure and recognizes the importance of context and provenance.

### Where the Policy Could Fail

The University cannot fully control what happens to content after publication.

The disclosure may disappear through cropping or screen recording, while the remaining audio and video continue to circulate.

### Residual Risk

The policy controls the University's distribution practices, but not the entire downstream information environment.

### Additional Control

Where practical, high-risk public synthetic media should contain disclosure within the visual or spoken content itself rather than relying only on external descriptions or metadata.

---

# 5. Provenance Is Available at Creation but Lost Later

### Scenario

A synthetic artifact is generated and retains available provenance information. It is later edited in another application and exported in a format that does not preserve the original provenance information.

### Policy Control

The policy requires the organization to preserve available provenance and to document when provenance is lost during a workflow.

### Where the Policy Could Fail

The Communications Office may have preserved the original provenance record while a downstream copy no longer contains it.

A viewer who encounters only the derivative copy may have no access to the original provenance information.

### Residual Risk

Provenance is strongest when multiple systems preserve and expose it consistently. The policy cannot force every external platform or editing workflow to retain it.

### Additional Control

The University should retain the original source and provenance record internally and use visible disclosure as an additional layer rather than treating provenance as the only safeguard.

---

# 6. Detection Fails or Produces an Uncertain Result

### Scenario

A synthetic video is flagged by an automated detector with a low-confidence result.

One employee interprets the result as proof that the video is authentic. Another interprets it as proof that it is fake.

### Policy Control

The policy treats automated systems as safeguards rather than as the sole basis for approval.

### Where the Policy Could Fail

Employees may misunderstand what an AI detector's score means or treat the output as a binary authenticity judgment.

### Residual Risk

Detection systems can produce uncertainty, false positives, false negatives, or results that are difficult for non-specialists to interpret.

### Additional Control

For high-risk projects, detection should be treated as supporting evidence. Reviewers should verify the source, approval record, and underlying content rather than relying on a detector score alone.

---

# 7. High-Volume Generation Multiplies a Small Error

### Scenario

The Communications Office produces 2,000 personalized synthetic videos for prospective students.

A source spreadsheet contains an incorrect deadline.

The generation workflow automatically inserts the incorrect date into hundreds of videos before the issue is detected.

### Policy Control

The policy requires human accountability, risk classification, and a pre-publication review process.

### Where the Policy Could Fail

Reviewing thousands of individually generated outputs may be impractical.

A team may reasonably rely on source-data validation, automated checks, sampling, or batch review.

### Residual Risk

Synthetic media introduces a **scale effect**: one upstream error can be multiplied across a large audience.

### Additional Control

High-volume Tier 2 and Tier 3 projects should include:

- source-data validation before generation;
- automated consistency checks;
- representative sampling;
- staged release;
- the ability to pause distribution quickly.

---

# 8. An Employee Uses an Unapproved AI Tool

### Scenario

A staff member is under a deadline and uses a free consumer AI platform because the University's approved tool is slower or unavailable.

The staff member uploads a person's photograph to create an avatar.

### Policy Control

The policy requires approved AI tools and prohibits bypassing the approval process by moving a rejected or unapproved project to another service.

### Where the Policy Could Fail

A policy cannot technically prevent every employee from accessing public AI services.

A staff member may also believe that using an external tool is acceptable because the content itself is not confidential.

### Residual Risk

Tool governance partly depends on training, procurement controls, approved-tool lists, and organizational culture.

### Additional Control

The Communications Office should maintain a current approved-tool list and provide employees with a simple escalation path when they need a capability not currently supported.

---

# 9. A High-Risk Communication Is Needed During a Crisis

### Scenario

A major campus emergency occurs while a senior university leader is unavailable.

Someone proposes using a synthetic avatar of that leader to release the emergency message because the process would be faster.

### Policy Control

The policy classifies emergency and public-safety communications as Tier 4 and prohibits the use of synthetic representations for those communications.

### Where the Policy Could Fail

Under time pressure, employees may believe that the policy should be bypassed because speed is more important than the normal review process.

### Residual Risk

A refusal policy is only credible if the organization has a practical alternative.

### Additional Control

The University's crisis-communications plan should specify authorized human spokespersons and backup communication methods so staff are not forced to choose between synthetic impersonation and delayed communication.

---

# 10. A Bad-Faith Actor Deliberately Circumvents the Policy

### Scenario

An employee intentionally creates prohibited synthetic media using a personal account and personal device, then distributes it anonymously.

### Policy Control

The policy clearly prohibits deceptive impersonation, non-consensual identity reproduction, and bypassing the approval process.

### Where the Policy Could Fail

Policies do not physically prevent deliberate misconduct.

The organization may also face difficulty identifying who created the artifact, particularly when external platforms and personal devices are involved.

### Residual Risk

Governance reduces organizational risk but does not eliminate the possibility of intentional abuse.

### Additional Control

The organization should combine the policy with employee training, clear disciplinary procedures, incident reporting, and auditing where appropriate.

---

# 11. Disclosure Exists but Does Not Explain Enough

### Scenario

A video contains the disclosure:

> AI-Generated Synthetic Media

A viewer correctly understands that AI was used but assumes that the real person shown approved the exact words in the video.

### Policy Control

The policy allows more specific disclosure when a real person's likeness is used, such as:

> AI-generated avatar. The person depicted did not record this video.

### Where the Policy Could Fail

A generic AI label does not necessarily explain:

- whether the person participated;
- whether the words were approved;
- whether the person's voice was cloned;
- whether the entire scene was generated.

### Residual Risk

Disclosure quality matters, not merely disclosure existence.

### Additional Control

Higher-risk projects should use specific disclosures that describe the relationship between the real person and the synthetic artifact.

---

# 12. Approval Becomes a Checklist Exercise

### Scenario

A Tier 3 project passes every checkbox:

- consent exists;
- disclosure exists;
- vendor is approved;
- provenance is available;
- reviewer signs off.

However, the content is still inappropriate because the synthetic representation gives the message a degree of authority that the audience would not otherwise assume.

### Policy Control

The policy requires human review and allows refusal when risk cannot reasonably be controlled.

### Where the Policy Could Fail

Reviewers may focus on compliance with individual controls rather than asking the larger question:

> Should the organization use synthetic media for this communication at all?

### Residual Risk

A sophisticated governance process can still become procedural if reviewers focus on completion rather than judgment.

### Additional Control

Reviewers should explicitly answer one qualitative question for Tier 3 projects:

> Would the communication still be appropriate if the audience fully understood how the synthetic representation was created and why it was used?

If the answer is uncertain, the project should be escalated or refused.

---

# 13. The Policy Relies on Good-Faith Behavior

The policy assumes that employees will:

- report projects accurately;
- obtain consent before generation;
- use approved tools;
- preserve required records;
- maintain disclosure;
- follow approval workflows;
- report incidents;
- and stop distribution when instructed.

This is a genuine weakness.

No written policy can guarantee that every employee will comply, particularly when deadlines, convenience, or incentives encourage shortcuts.

However, relying on good faith is not necessarily a reason to abandon policy. The purpose of a governance document is to establish clear expectations, assign responsibility, make violations identifiable, and create an institutional response when controls fail.

The policy should therefore be paired with training, periodic audits, clear ownership, and proportionate consequences for deliberate violations.

---

# 14. Residual Risk Summary

Even after applying the policy, the following risks remain:

| Residual Risk | Why the Policy Cannot Eliminate It |
|---|---|
| Context loss | Third parties control downstream copies |
| Provenance loss | External tools may not preserve metadata |
| Detection uncertainty | Automated detectors are not definitive |
| Consent disputes | People may interpret scope or reuse differently |
| Scale errors | Automated workflows can multiply mistakes |
| Tool circumvention | Employees can access external services |
| Deliberate misuse | Policies cannot physically stop bad actors |
| Misinterpretation | Disclosure does not guarantee audience understanding |
| Checklist behavior | Compliance does not guarantee ethical judgment |
| Post-publication manipulation | External parties can edit or reframe content |

---

# 15. Final Stress-Test Assessment

The policy is strongest when synthetic-media use is planned, the Request Owner is identifiable, the audience is known, the represented people have provided appropriate consent, and the University controls the publication environment.

It becomes weaker when:

- synthetic media is produced under extreme time pressure;
- large numbers of artifacts are generated automatically;
- content moves across multiple platforms;
- consent changes after publication;
- third parties alter or reframe the content;
- employees use unapproved tools;
- or a bad-faith actor intentionally circumvents controls.

The central limitation is that the policy governs the University's **production and distribution process**, but it cannot control every later use of the resulting media.

The most realistic goal is therefore not zero risk. It is **bounded and accountable risk**:

- low-risk uses should be made easy to perform correctly;
- high-risk uses should require stronger evidence and review;
- prohibited uses should have a clear refusal path;
- incidents should trigger a defined response; and
- the organization should maintain enough records to understand what happened when a control fails.

The policy should also be reviewed periodically because synthetic-media tools, platform capabilities, provenance standards, and organizational use cases will continue to change.
