# Bonus 1 — Cross-Context Policy Comparison

## University Communications Office vs. Corporate Communications & Marketing

The primary Phase B policy was written for a University Communications Office. A second policy was developed for a Corporate Communications & Marketing function to test which governance controls remain useful across organizations and which must change with context.

The comparison shows that the core governance framework is portable, while the specific risk triggers, audiences, and approval thresholds are context-dependent.

---

## 1. Areas Where the Policies Converge

### Consent

Both contexts require documented consent before using a recognizable person's synthetic likeness or voice.

This is portable because the underlying issue is the individual's control over their identity rather than the type of organization.

### Disclosure

Both policies require clear disclosure when synthetic media could otherwise be mistaken for authentic human-created media.

The wording can change by use case, but the principle is broadly transferable.

### Approved AI Vendors

Both policies restrict the use of unapproved AI services for identity-bearing or organizational content.

This is portable because employee convenience does not remove privacy, security, or governance obligations.

### Human Accountability

Both policies assign responsibility to a human Request Owner and reviewer instead of treating the AI system as the accountable party.

### Provenance and Records

Both policies encourage preservation of original files, generation information, approvals, consent, and provenance where available.

### Incident Response

Both policies include containment, evidence preservation, notification, correction, and post-incident review.

### Explicit Refusal

Both policies define uses that should be refused rather than allowing every use through disclosure alone.

---

## 2. Areas Where the Policies Diverge

| Governance Area | University Context | Corporate Context |
|---|---|---|
| Primary audience | Students, faculty, staff, applicants, donors, public | Customers, employees, investors, partners, public |
| High-impact communications | Safety, disciplinary decisions, university leadership, student-facing announcements | Financial/investor communications, product safety, executive statements, regulatory notices |
| Consent concerns | Faculty, staff, students, administrators | Employees, executives, customers, partners |
| Marketing use | Recruitment, admissions, alumni, donor communications | Brand campaigns, product marketing, customer communications |
| Crisis communications | Campus emergencies and public safety | Product crises, recalls, executive crises, operational incidents |
| Financial risk | Donor and institutional communications | Investor, earnings, market-sensitive communications |
| Academic context | Education and research demonstrations | Training, sales enablement, product education |

---

## 3. What the Divergence Tells Us

The policies suggest that synthetic-media governance has two layers.

### Portable controls

Some controls should travel across almost any organization:

- consent;
- disclosure;
- approved tools;
- human review;
- provenance;
- audit records;
- incident response;
- explicit refusal;
- accountable ownership.

These controls address structural properties of synthetic media rather than industry-specific outcomes.

### Context-specific controls

Other controls depend heavily on what the organization communicates and who relies on it.

A university needs stronger controls around student/faculty representation, admissions, donor communications, and campus safety.

A corporation needs stronger controls around executive statements, customer communications, employment decisions, financial information, product safety, and regulatory communications.

This means a single generic synthetic-media policy is likely to be too vague in high-impact environments. A common governance framework can be reused, but risk tiers and prohibited uses should be adapted to the organization's actual operations.

---

## 4. Which Policy Is More Restrictive?

The comparison should not be interpreted as one organization requiring universally stronger controls.

Instead, the policies are restrictive in different places.

For example:

- A university may need additional safeguards for student and academic communications.
- A corporation may need additional safeguards for investor, executive, product-safety, and market-sensitive communications.

The appropriate controls therefore follow the **consequences of misrepresentation**, not simply the size or type of organization.

---

## 5. Governance Portability Model

A useful way to transfer the policy framework between industries is:

**Common governance layer**

1. Risk classification
2. Consent
3. Disclosure
4. Approved vendor
5. Provenance
6. Human review
7. Audit trail
8. Incident response
9. Refusal

+

**Context-specific layer**

10. High-impact communication categories
11. Required approvers
12. Audience protections
13. Retention requirements
14. Escalation rules
15. Industry-specific prohibited uses

This two-layer model allows organizations to adopt consistent baseline controls without pretending that every synthetic-media risk is identical.

---

## 6. Key Finding

The comparison suggests that **governance principles are more portable than governance thresholds**.

The same question should be asked everywhere:

> Who is being represented, what is being communicated, who could rely on it, and what happens if the representation is wrong or unauthorized?

But the answer to "what requires escalation or refusal?" depends on the organization.

That is why synthetic-media governance should be standardized at the control level while remaining context-specific at the risk and approval level.
