Phase A — Ethical Analysis
1. Returning to What I Built

Task 6 changed my understanding of synthetic media primarily because of how easy the technology was to use. Creating the ElevenLabs audio required very little technical knowledge or effort. The platform provided multiple voice options, including options available through the free version, and a usable narration could be generated within seconds. The experience showed me that producing a synthetic voice is no longer a technically complicated process reserved for specialists.

HeyGen produced a similar experience on the video side. The platform provided several ways to customize the output, including the choice of avatar, narration, video length, visual presentation, and whether to use an existing avatar or create one from a personal photograph. The interface also asked me questions about the intended style and presentation before generating the video. This gave me a significant amount of control over the final artifact without requiring advanced video-production skills.

However, the difference between accessibility and realism became clear when I compared the two outputs. The ElevenLabs audio was relatively convincing and natural in its pacing and delivery. The HeyGen video was much easier for me to identify as synthetic. The facial expressions and lip synchronization were reasonably effective, but the avatar's posture, positioning, and head movement were less natural. Because I was the person who created the artifact, I was able to notice these inconsistencies easily. This made me realize that synthetic-media systems can be very accessible while still having noticeable limitations, particularly in the free versions I tested.

The experience also changed how I think about consent. Using my own photograph to create the Arsh avatar felt fundamentally different from using another person's face or voice. I had knowingly provided my own image for this purpose and therefore had control over how my likeness was being used. If the same process were applied to another person's photograph or voice, I would expect explicit permission before creating or distributing the resulting media. In a real organizational setting, that consent would ideally be documented through a formal agreement that clearly explains how the person's likeness or voice will be used, where it may appear, and whether compensation is involved.

The audio experiment also demonstrated why synthetic media can become powerful even when the technology is not perfect. I found the voice more convincing when the generated speech matched the characteristics expected from the speaker. This suggests that the combination of a plausible voice and believable contextual information can make synthetic audio more persuasive. In comparison, the avatar video I created was less convincing because the facial expressions, posture, and movement did not fully resemble natural human behavior.

Most importantly, Task 6 made me more cautious about the personal data involved in synthetic-media systems. I would not stop using tools such as ElevenLabs or HeyGen, because they provide useful capabilities and are relatively convenient. However, I would be more careful about uploading my photograph, voice, or other identifying material to these platforms. The fact that a tool can generate a useful artifact does not eliminate the question of what happens to the source material used to create it. My experience therefore shifted my perspective from simply asking whether a synthetic-media tool can produce a convincing result to also asking whether I am comfortable providing the underlying personal material required to produce that result.

Reflection

The most important lesson from Task 6 was that the ethical issue is not limited to whether the final artifact looks or sounds real. The tools made synthetic representation easy to create, but that ease also lowers the barrier to misuse. My own experiment was harmless because the content was truthful and I used my own likeness. Changing either of those conditions could fundamentally change the ethical situation. This is the starting point for the thought experiments in the next section.

2. Truth Axis: From Verified Analysis to Fabricated Claims

My experience in Task 6 began with a relatively harmless use of synthetic media. I took an analytical narrative from Task 5 that had been grounded in a real Premier League dataset and independently validated, and I converted that narrative into synthetic audio using ElevenLabs and a synthetic video using HeyGen. The ElevenLabs narration was surprisingly easy to produce and sounded natural enough to resemble a conversational explanation. HeyGen also made it possible to create a video using my own avatar with relatively little technical effort. Although the video had noticeable limitations in head movement, positioning, and lip synchronization, the overall process demonstrated that a person with limited technical expertise can create synthetic media quickly.

That experience becomes ethically different when the underlying content changes from truthful to false. In Task 6, the synthetic medium was only a different way of communicating information that I had already verified. The same tools could instead be used to present information that the creator knows is inaccurate.

Consider a business scenario in which an employee creates a short AI-generated video using an executive-style avatar and synthetic voice. The video states that the company's quarterly revenue increased by 25% and that customer demand is accelerating. In reality, the company's revenue has declined. The creator knows that the information is false but uses a realistic-looking presentation because the message is more likely to be trusted when it appears to come from an authoritative business representative.

The ethical concern here is greater than simply publishing a false statement. Synthetic media can make the false statement appear to have a speaker, authority, and personal endorsement that do not actually exist. A viewer may not perceive the message as an anonymous claim; instead, they may believe that the executive shown in the video personally made the statement. The medium therefore has the potential to add credibility to information that would otherwise receive greater scrutiny.

The consequences could extend beyond misinformation. Employees could make operational decisions based on the fabricated announcement. Customers or business partners could interpret the message as an official company communication. Investors could react to the apparent financial information. Even if the synthetic video were eventually identified, correcting the original claim might not reverse the decisions made by people who saw it first.

My Task 6 experience also highlighted an important imbalance between production and verification. Producing the ElevenLabs audio required only seconds, and creating the HeyGen video required relatively little technical effort. Verifying a suspicious business announcement, however, could require considerably more time. A person would need to locate the company's actual financial information, determine whether the speaker really made the statement, and potentially compare the content with official communications. This creates an asymmetry in which false synthetic content can be produced quickly while establishing the truth may take substantially longer.

Disclosure also does not completely resolve this problem. Suppose the original video contains an AI-generated label, but someone later removes the label, crops the video, or reposts only part of the recording. The fabricated business claim could then circulate separately from the information that identified it as synthetic. A viewer encountering the shortened version might interpret it as an authentic corporate statement.

This thought experiment changes how I think about the role of synthetic media. The ethical problem is not simply that AI can generate false information. The more important issue is that synthetic representation can give false information an appearance of personal authority and authenticity. A fictional example clearly presented as fiction is different from a fabricated business announcement designed to make viewers believe that a real executive said something that never happened.

Ethical Boundary

For me, the critical boundary is whether synthetic media is being used to misrepresent both the truth of the message and the apparent source of that message. A synthetic avatar explaining a fictional scenario for educational purposes may be acceptable when its nature is clear. A synthetic avatar presenting fabricated financial information as though it were an authentic statement from an executive crosses a much more serious ethical boundary.

My Task 6 experiment therefore changed the question I would ask before creating synthetic media. Instead of asking only whether the artifact can be made convincing, I would first ask:

Is the information truthful, and does the synthetic representation give that information an authority or endorsement that it does not legitimately possess?

The more accessible and convincing synthetic media becomes, the more important those questions become.

Consent Axis: From Voluntary Representation to Non-Consensual Use

My experience in Task 6 made the importance of consent more concrete because I used my own photograph to create the Arsh avatar in HeyGen. I knowingly uploaded my image and gave the platform permission to use my likeness for the purpose of the experiment. Because the avatar represented me and I understood how it was being used, I was comfortable with the resulting synthetic video. Even though the output was imperfect and clearly AI-generated, I retained control over the decision to participate.

The ethical situation changes when the same process is applied to another person without their permission. Imagine that an organization has a senior executive whose photograph and publicly available interviews are easily accessible online. An employee uses those materials to create a synthetic avatar and voice that closely resemble the executive. The employee then creates a short video announcing a new company policy. The policy itself is real, and the information in the video is accurate, but the executive never agreed to have their likeness or voice digitally reproduced.

At first, this situation may appear less harmful than the fabricated financial announcement in the Truth scenario because the information being communicated is truthful. However, the ethical problem has shifted from what is being said to who is being represented as saying it.

The synthetic video creates an apparent connection between the executive and a message that the executive did not actually record or approve. Viewers may reasonably assume that the executive personally participated in the communication because the face and voice are familiar. Even if the employee's intentions are positive, the representation removes an important element of personal agency: the person being depicted no longer controls when, how, or in what context their identity is used.

This matters because a person's voice and likeness are not simply visual or audio assets. They are closely associated with identity, reputation, and authority. In my Task 6 experiment, using my own avatar felt acceptable because I was making the decision about my own representation. If I had instead uploaded someone else's photograph and generated a video without asking them, I would no longer be making the decision only for myself. I would be imposing a representation on another person who had no opportunity to review, approve, or reject it.

What should consent involve?

For an organizational setting, I do not think consent should simply mean receiving an informal verbal "yes." A responsible process should establish what the person is agreeing to and how their likeness or voice can be used.

For example, a consent agreement could specify:

What voice, image, or likeness will be reproduced.
The specific purpose for which it may be used.
Where the resulting synthetic media may be distributed.
How long the permission remains valid.
Whether the person can revoke permission.
Whether the person receives compensation when the likeness is being used for organizational or commercial purposes.

This becomes especially important if the synthetic representation could be reused beyond the original project. A person might agree to an internal training video but not want the same avatar used in advertising or social media. Consent therefore needs to be specific to the intended use, rather than treated as unlimited permission.

The scale of the problem

The risk also increases when synthetic representation becomes reusable. Suppose an employee provides consent for one five-minute training video, but the organization retains the person's voice model or avatar and later uses it for dozens of other communications without asking again. The original consent may have been legitimate, but the later uses could exceed what the person reasonably understood they had agreed to.

This creates an important distinction between consent to create and consent to reuse. Agreeing to the initial generation does not automatically mean agreeing to every future application of the resulting synthetic representation.

Where I would draw the boundary

My experience in Task 6 leads me to a fairly clear position: a person's likeness or voice should not be synthetically reproduced for organizational communication without explicit permission from that person.

There may be legitimate exceptions where an organization needs to use a synthetic representation for accessibility, continuity, or other practical reasons, but those uses should still require documented authorization and clear disclosure.

I would also distinguish between permission to use someone's existing photograph or recording and permission to create a reusable synthetic representation of them. The latter is potentially more consequential because once a voice or avatar has been created, it can be reused to produce new messages that the person never personally recorded.

The central ethical issue therefore is not simply whether the representation is accurate. It is whether the person being represented has agency over that representation.

My Task 6 experience made that distinction clear to me. Using my own face felt fundamentally different from using someone else's because I remained the person deciding how my identity was represented. That same principle should apply in an organizational setting: the closer synthetic media comes to reproducing a real person's identity, the stronger the requirement for explicit, informed, and documented consent should become.

This also leads naturally into the next axis. Even when the content is truthful and the person has consented, the ethical situation can still change depending on where the synthetic media appears, how the disclosure travels with it, and what context the audience encounters it in. That is the Context Axis.

Context Axis: When the Meaning Changes After Distribution

My Task 6 artifact was created with a specific context in mind. The HeyGen video was intended as a professional presentation of my research, and the generated media was connected to a clearly defined project about synthetic-media experimentation. Within that context, the viewer has information about why the video exists, who created it, and what it is intended to demonstrate. The synthetic nature of the video is also part of the surrounding documentation.

The ethical situation changes when the same artifact is removed from that context.

Imagine that the 45-second HeyGen video I created is downloaded or screen-recorded and then edited into a ten-second clip. The original project description and surrounding explanation are removed. The clip is posted on social media with a caption implying that I personally recorded and published the statement. The video itself has not necessarily been altered in terms of the words being spoken, but the context surrounding it has changed.

The viewer encountering the shortened version may have no knowledge that it was produced as a synthetic-media experiment. The original purpose of the video—to demonstrate the capabilities and limitations of AI-generated media—has disappeared. What remains is an apparently authentic person making a statement.

This demonstrates that context can function as part of the meaning of synthetic media. A synthetic artifact does not exist in isolation. Its interpretation depends on where it appears, what information accompanies it, how much of the original artifact remains, and what assumptions the audience brings to it.

Disclosure is vulnerable to separation

My Task 6 experience also made me question how reliable disclosure is once content leaves its original environment. A creator can label a video clearly, but that label may not remain attached to every copy of the content. A clip can be cropped, re-recorded, compressed, or embedded inside another piece of content. Even if the original artifact contains a watermark or disclosure, a derivative version may not.

This creates an important distinction between disclosing synthetic content at the point of creation and ensuring that the disclosure survives distribution.

For example, suppose a synthetic video contains an obvious label stating "AI-Generated Synthetic Interview." On the original HeyGen output, the viewer can understand that the speaker is an artificial representation. But if someone screen-records the video and crops away the label, the same visual content can circulate without the information that explained what it was.

The underlying media has not become more authentic, but the audience's interpretation of it can change dramatically.

Recontextualization can create a false impression

The problem becomes even more serious when a synthetic clip is combined with unrelated information.

Imagine that someone takes a ten-second segment from my research video and posts it alongside a caption claiming that I made a particular statement about a company, political issue, or financial event. The words in the original recording might be completely different in meaning when placed next to that caption.

This creates a form of manipulation that does not necessarily require generating a new synthetic artifact. The existing artifact can be reframed to communicate a meaning that the creator never intended.

That observation is important because it means that synthetic-media governance cannot focus exclusively on how the media was generated. The organization also needs to consider how the media can be distributed, excerpted, embedded, and interpreted after creation.

Provenance helps, but it is not the same as context

This also raises the role of provenance. A content credential or other provenance mechanism may provide information about where a piece of media originated, but provenance does not automatically tell every viewer how the content is being used or whether an accompanying claim is misleading.

A viewer could potentially know that a video originated from an AI-generation platform and still misunderstand what the video means because of the caption, surrounding discussion, or selective editing.

Therefore, provenance and disclosure should be treated as layers of protection rather than complete solutions.

Where I would draw the boundary

My Task 6 experience leads me to believe that responsible use requires thinking beyond the original artifact. A creator should consider not only whether the synthetic media is clearly identified at the moment it is produced, but also whether a reasonable viewer could encounter an excerpt or derivative version without that information.

For organizational use, this suggests that sensitive synthetic media should be designed with redistribution in mind. Important disclosures should be visible within the content itself where practical, rather than relying exclusively on metadata or accompanying documentation.

The central ethical issue on the Context axis is therefore not simply:

"Was the synthetic media labeled?"

It is:

"Will a person who encounters the media outside its original context still have enough information to understand what they are seeing?"

Task 6 showed me that creating synthetic media is only the beginning of the process. Once the artifact leaves the creator's control, its meaning can be influenced by editing, cropping, reposting, and surrounding claims. Responsible governance therefore needs to account for the life of synthetic media after creation, not just the moment when the "Generate" button is pressed.

Scale Axis: From One Artifact to Mass Production

In Task 6, I created only two synthetic-media artifacts: one ElevenLabs audio recording and one HeyGen video. The process required relatively little time or technical expertise. The audio could be generated within seconds, while the video required more setup but was still accessible through a browser-based interface. Working at this small scale made the technology feel manageable because I was directly involved in every step and could inspect the output myself.

The ethical implications change when the same capability is scaled beyond an individual experiment. Imagine a company using synthetic-media tools to produce personalized communications for thousands of customers. Instead of recording one announcement, the organization generates hundreds or thousands of slightly different videos, each featuring a synthetic spokesperson and customized information for a particular recipient.

At first glance, this could be a legitimate use of automation. A company might use synthetic media to provide individualized onboarding instructions, product explanations, or training materials. The efficiency could be significant because creating thousands of traditional videos would require substantial time and production resources.

However, scale also changes the potential for harm. When only one synthetic artifact is produced, a human reviewer can reasonably watch it, verify the claims, check the disclosure, and confirm that the likeness and voice have been authorized. Reviewing thousands of generated artifacts manually becomes much more difficult. The organization may therefore begin relying on automated checks or sampling rather than reviewing every output.

This creates a new risk: small errors that would be caught in a single artifact can become systemic errors when production is automated. A pronunciation mistake, incorrect data field, inappropriate statement, or missing disclosure might appear in hundreds of outputs before anyone notices. The problem is no longer simply that one piece of synthetic media is wrong; the production system itself can multiply the mistake.

Scale also changes the potential impact of malicious use. A person attempting to deceive others no longer needs to spend significant time producing each individual piece of misinformation. Once a workflow has been established, the same synthetic voice or avatar can potentially be used repeatedly. A bad actor could produce different versions of a message targeted at different audiences, making the deception more difficult to recognize as a coordinated campaign.

This is where my Task 6 experience becomes particularly important. The barrier to entry was surprisingly low. I did not need advanced programming knowledge, specialized hardware, or professional video-production skills to create the artifacts. The platforms handled most of the difficult technical work. That means the limiting factor at scale may not be technical capability but rather time, cost, and organizational willingness to use the tools.

The distinction between generating one artifact and generating thousands therefore changes the governance problem. At small scale, individual judgment can provide much of the oversight. At large scale, an organization needs systems that make responsible behavior repeatable. A policy cannot rely entirely on an employee remembering to check every output because the efficiency advantage of synthetic media comes partly from reducing manual effort.

Scale and accountability

Scale can also make accountability less obvious. Suppose a company distributes 10,000 personalized synthetic videos and one contains a serious error. It may be difficult to determine whether the problem came from the original script, the generation system, the personalization data, the employee who approved the campaign, or an automated process that modified the content later.

This suggests that responsible use at scale requires more than obtaining permission to use synthetic media. Organizations also need to know who is accountable for the outputs produced by the system.

A useful principle would be that automation should increase the efficiency of production without removing human responsibility for the resulting content. The fact that an AI system generated a message should not become an excuse for the organization to avoid accountability when the message causes harm.

Where I would draw the boundary

My view is that scale should increase the level of oversight rather than reduce it. A low-risk internal training video and a campaign distributing thousands of synthetic customer-facing messages should not go through exactly the same approval process.

The larger the audience, the greater the potential consequence of a mistake, and the more important it becomes to have structured review, logging, consent records, disclosure, and mechanisms for stopping distribution when a problem is discovered.

The central ethical question on the Scale axis is therefore:

What happens when a capability that is relatively harmless at the scale of one experiment becomes powerful enough to automate persuasion or misrepresentation at the scale of thousands?

Task 6 showed me that the technology is already accessible at an individual level. The ethical challenge at scale is making sure that greater production efficiency does not become greater efficiency at producing harm.

6. Mitigation Landscape

My experience in Task 6 showed me that synthetic media can be created with very little technical effort. That makes mitigation important, but it also showed me that no single safeguard can completely address the risks. My ElevenLabs audio sounded natural to me, yet an automated detector classified it as 98% AI-generated. My HeyGen video contained a platform watermark, but the underlying video was still recognizable as synthetic through its facial movement and positioning. These experiences suggest that responsible synthetic-media governance needs multiple layers rather than reliance on one technical mechanism.

Disclosure

Disclosure is one of the simplest ways to inform audiences that media has been generated or meaningfully altered by AI. It can take the form of an on-screen label, spoken acknowledgment, platform-generated notice, or watermark.

My Task 6 experience showed me why disclosure matters. I did not include an explicit spoken or on-screen statement that my audio and video were AI-generated. The synthetic nature of the work was instead documented in the surrounding project materials. This would be adequate for someone viewing the GitHub repository, but it would not necessarily be adequate for someone encountering the media independently.

Current platform practices demonstrate that disclosure is becoming an important part of synthetic-media governance. YouTube requires creators to disclose realistic AI-generated or meaningfully altered content, including content that makes a real person appear to say or do something they did not actually do. YouTube can also apply labels based on creator disclosures, its own detection systems, or valid C2PA Content Credentials.

The weakness of disclosure is that the label may not remain connected to the media. A video can be cropped, screen-recorded, edited, or reposted elsewhere. A watermark can also be treated as a visual decoration rather than meaningful information about provenance. Disclosure therefore works best when it is clear, prominent, and difficult to separate from the content itself.

Disclosure also does not answer whether the underlying statement is true. A clearly labeled synthetic video can still contain false information, improperly use someone's likeness, or deliberately mislead its audience. Disclosure should therefore be viewed as a transparency mechanism rather than permission to use synthetic media for any purpose.

Provenance and Content Credentials

Provenance addresses a different problem. Rather than relying solely on what a viewer can see, systems such as C2PA Content Credentials can record information about the history of a digital asset, including how it was created and modified. C2PA describes Content Credentials as cryptographically signed and tamper-evident provenance information that can travel with an asset.

The advantage is that provenance can provide evidence about an artifact's origin even when its visual appearance does not reveal much. This can be particularly useful for journalists, organizations, platforms, and investigators trying to understand where a piece of media came from.

However, provenance has an important limitation: provenance is not the same thing as truth. A credential can establish information about the content's creation history without proving that the claims made inside the content are accurate. An AI-generated video containing a false statement could still have perfectly valid provenance information showing that it was generated by an AI system.

There is also a practical dependency on adoption. Provenance is most useful when generation tools, editing tools, platforms, and viewers all support compatible standards. If credentials are unavailable, ignored, or separated from the content, the viewer may have no practical way to use them.

I did not test C2PA in Task 6, so I cannot claim that my own artifact demonstrated how its credentials survive re-encoding or reposting. Instead, my Task 6 experience with HeyGen's watermark and restricted export made me aware of the broader issue: information about how content was created is only useful if that information remains available to the person evaluating the content.

Detection

Detection attempts to determine whether a piece of media was generated or altered by AI. My own experiment provided direct evidence that detection can sometimes identify synthetic content that appears natural to a human listener.

The ElevenLabs audio received a 98% AI-generated classification from the detector I used. This was interesting because I personally found the narration natural and appropriately paced. The detection result therefore demonstrated that subjective realism and automated detectability are not the same thing.

The FTC has also approached voice-cloning risk as a problem requiring multiple intervention points, including upstream prevention or authentication, real-time detection, and post-use evaluation. In discussing its Voice Cloning Challenge, the FTC explicitly noted that there is no single solution to the problem.

Detection nevertheless has limitations. A confidence score does not necessarily explain exactly why a system reached its conclusion, and detection systems must operate against generators that continue to change. A detector that performs well on one generator or generation method may not perform equally well on another.

Detection also places some of the burden on the audience or investigator. If synthetic media can be created in seconds but determining whether it is authentic requires specialized analysis, that imbalance can make detection a useful secondary safeguard without making it a complete solution.

For an organization, I would therefore treat detection as one layer of verification, not as an automatic final judgment.

Legal and Regulatory Approaches

Governments are addressing synthetic media through several overlapping approaches rather than one universal synthetic-media law. These include transparency requirements, consumer-protection rules, restrictions related to impersonation, election-related rules, and protections concerning personal likeness and non-consensual uses.

In the European Union, the AI Act's transparency framework includes obligations concerning certain AI-generated or manipulated content, including deepfakes. This reflects a regulatory approach in which people should be informed when realistic synthetic content could otherwise be mistaken for authentic material.

In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission's existing Government and Business Impersonation Rule prohibits materially and falsely posing as a government entity, officer, business, or business officer, or falsely misrepresenting affiliation. The FTC has also pursued broader work addressing AI-enabled impersonation and voice cloning.

The strength of regulation is that it can establish enforceable minimum standards rather than relying entirely on voluntary behavior. Its weakness is that laws tend to address particular categories of harm and jurisdictions separately. Synthetic media can cross borders quickly, while legal rules and enforcement mechanisms remain jurisdiction-specific.

Regulation also cannot answer every organizational question. A use can be technically lawful while still being inappropriate for a university, newsroom, or business. That is why organizational policies need to establish standards that may be stricter than the legal minimum.

Platform Policy

Platforms have become an important layer of governance because they control how synthetic content is labeled, distributed, reported, and sometimes removed.

YouTube, for example, requires disclosure of realistic AI-generated or meaningfully altered content and states that it may automatically apply AI-related labels in some circumstances. Its policies also provide mechanisms for people to report realistic synthetic content that uses their likeness.

Meta has similarly been developing labeling and provenance approaches and stated in July 2026 that it was signing the EU AI Act Code of Practice on transparency for AI-generated content. Meta also acknowledged the challenge of making labels useful without overwhelming users with inconsistent disclosure systems.

Platform policy provides an important distribution-layer safeguard, but there are obvious weaknesses. Policies differ between platforms, enforcement can be imperfect, and a piece of content can move between platforms where different standards apply. A synthetic artifact that is correctly labeled on one platform may appear differently when reposted elsewhere.

There is also an important distinction between platform enforcement and ethical judgment. A platform allowing content to remain online does not necessarily mean the content is ethically appropriate, just as removal from one platform does not necessarily establish that the underlying claim is false.

Professional and Organizational Norms

Professional organizations can provide standards that go beyond legal compliance. A useful example is the Associated Press, which currently allows certain limited uses of generative AI while retaining human responsibility for editorial judgment, verification, and accountability. AP's updated newsroom standards state that AI output must be reviewed and edited by journalists and that AI does not replace reporting, sourcing, editorial judgment, or verification.

AP's approach is particularly relevant to this project because it treats human accountability as separate from the tool. The fact that an AI system produced an output does not transfer responsibility for that output to the software provider.

Organizational norms can therefore address questions that platform rules and laws cannot easily resolve. An organization can decide that certain uses are too sensitive even when they are technically possible or legally permissible. It can also require documented consent, human review, disclosure, provenance, and approval before synthetic media reaches an audience.

The limitation is that organizational norms depend heavily on implementation. A well-written policy does little if employees do not understand it, managers do not enforce it, or deadlines create incentives to bypass review.

Overall Assessment

The mitigation landscape suggests that synthetic-media governance works best as a layered system.

Disclosure helps the audience understand what they are seeing. Provenance can provide evidence about where media came from. Detection can identify suspicious artifacts. Laws create enforceable boundaries. Platforms can apply labels and remove certain harmful content. Professional and organizational norms establish standards for responsible use.

But none of these mechanisms can independently guarantee that synthetic media is truthful, consensual, appropriately contextualized, or harmless.

That conclusion follows directly from my Task 6 experience. The audio could sound natural while still being detected as AI. The video could be generated easily while still containing visible artifacts. A watermark could identify the platform without explaining the purpose of the content. And a synthetic artifact could be responsibly created by one person but potentially misinterpreted after being separated from its original context.

For that reason, I would not design an organizational policy around the assumption that one mitigation will catch every problem. The more consequential the synthetic-media use, the more layers of review and protection should be required.
